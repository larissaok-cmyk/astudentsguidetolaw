The Declaration of Independence (1776)

Article written by A Student's Guide to Law. Last updated: June 29, 2026

The Declaration of Independence basically formally created the United States as a whole. Explore the historical context and its effects on our society.

What is now the United States of America had been previously colonized by Great Britain from 1607 to 1783, beginning with the colonization of Jamestown, Virginia and concluding with the end of the American Revolutionary War and the signing of the Treaty of Paris in 1783.

British powers often took advantage of the American colonies in many ways, some examples include forcing the colonies to export raw materials from their territory to the mother country (Britain) while taxing a lot on the manufactured goods they were forced to buy back. Colonists were also prohibited from trading goods with other countries that were not Britain.

A document (The Declaration of Independence) was then created to declare the 13 colonies fully independent from British powers, unify them, and secure new foreign alliances.

To do that, they needed to make the American colonies sovereign and capable of making treaties and waging war. This document was fueled by many Enlightenment principles, especially the ideas of popular sovereignty and the social contract. The document was written by Thomas Jefferson.

Now, you know why the Declaration of Independence was written. Let's look at its content and structure. The Declaration of Independence contains 5 parts:

  • The Introduction (Preamble): Talks about what the document is all about, declaring separation from the British Empire.
  • The Statement of Human Rights: Covered John Locke's Social Contract theory stating the right to "life, liberty, and happiness".
  • The Charges Against Human Rights: Covers the duty of people to overthrow or alter a destructive government.
  • The Statement of Separation: Detailed 27 abuses by King George III, such as tyranny and taxation without representation.
  • Conclusion (The Pledge): Declared independence, cut ties, and pledged lives, fortunes, and honor.

Today, the Declaration of Independence still inspires many liberational movements around the world.

The Articles of Confederation (1781)

Article written by A Student's Guide to Law. Last updated: August 15, 2026

The Articles of Confederation organized the U.S.'s government at the time and how the confederation would work. It had to be corrected, though!

The Articles of Confederation were the first constitution of the United States, drafted in 1777 and ratified in 1781. They made the centralized government extremely weak because of the fear of tyranny under Britain. Flaws included:

  • Congress could not collect taxes or control trade.
  • No way to force states to supply money or soldiers.
  • Nine out of 13 states had to agree to pass laws.
  • Unanimous vote required to amend the articles.

Shays's Rebellion

Shays’s rebellion was an armed uprising in western Massachusetts from 1786 to 1787 by farmers facing debt and high taxes. It highlighted the lack of a national army to respond to crises, prompting the Constitutional Convention of 1787.

Federalist 51 by James Madison

Article written by A Student's Guide to Law. Last updated: November 15, 2026

When the Articles of Confederation were drafted and ratified, new problems and debates emerged regarding federalism.

James Madison wrote Federalist 51 to explain how the structure of the new U.S. Constitution would prevent tyranny. He proposed that the Separation of Powers and Checks and Balances would keep each branch dependent yet independent enough to check one another.
