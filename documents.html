The Declaration of Independence (1776)

Article written by A Student's Guide to Law. Last updated: June 29, 2026

The Declaration of Independence basically formally created the United States as a whole. Explore the historical context and its effects on our society

What is now the United States of America had been previously colonized by Great Britain from 1607 to 1783, beginning with the colonization of Jamestown, Virginia and concluding with the end of the American Revolutionary War and the signing of the Treaty of Paris in 1783.

British powers often took advantage of the American colonies in many ways, some examples include forcing the colonies to export raw materials from their territory to the mother country (Britain) while taxing a lot on the manufactured goods (the ones that came out of British factories) they were forced to buy back. Colonists were also prohibited from trading goods with other countries that were not Britain, therefore only enriching themselves.

A document (The Declaration of Independence) was then created to declare the 13 colonies fully independent from British powers, unify them, and secure new foreign alliances.

To do that, they needed to make the American colonies sovereign and capable of making treaties and waging war. This document was fueled by many Enlightenment principles, especially the ideas of popular sovereignty and the social contract. The document was written by Thomas Jefferson.

Now, you know why the Declaration of Independence was written. Let's look at its content and structure.

The Declaration of Independence contains 5 parts:

  • The Introduction (Preamble): This part talks about what the document is all about, declaring that they would state why they would be separating from the British Empire and also then talking about how Independence was pretty much inevitable for them.
  • The Statement of Human Rights: Here, Enlightenment principles are all over the place. The Social Contract theory by John Locke was covered in stating the right of the people to "life, liberty, and happiness" and that governments receive their power from the governed, not from monarchs or divine rights.
  • The Charges Against Human Rights: Also influenced by the Social Contract theory, this section covers the idea that it was the duty of the people to overthrow or alter a government if it was not protecting their rights, which was what Great Britain was doing to them according to the document.
  • The Statement of Separation (list of grievances against King George III): Included in detail 27 abuses by King George, such as tyranny, taxation without representation, and waging wars without consent.
  • Conclusion (The Pledge): It declared the 13 colonies independent, officialy cut ties with Britain, and "pledges the signers' lives, fortune and honor". That same sentence finalized the Declaration of Independence text itself.

Today, the Declaration of Independence still inspires many liberational movements around the world.

The Articles of Confederation (1776)

Article written by A Student's Guide to Law. Last updated: August 15, 2026

The Articles of Confederation organized the U.S.'s government at the time and how the confederation would work. It had to be corrected, though!

The Articles of Confederation were the first constitution of the United States. The Articles, drafted in 1977 and ratified in 1781 set how the states would be organized and how the government would work. They had made the centralized government extremely weak because of the fear of tyranny they had once lived under the British. However, according to this list, that arrangement was not working well:

  • Congress could not collect taxes and control trade between states and other nations.
  • There was no way to make states forcibly supply money or soldiers.
  • To pass laws, nine out of 13 states had to agree.
  • To amend the articles, a full unanimous (every state had to agree) vote was required by the states. And if you know a little bit about life, it’s pretty hard to get everybody to agree on the same thing.

Shay's Rebellion

Shay’s rebellion was an armed uprising in western Massachusetts from 1786 to 1787. It was caused by farmers needing to pay their debts to the government. They were lacking cash to pay debts caused by big Massachusetts taxes to pay off war debts. Being jailed and loosing land, farmers uprose and seized weapons at the federal armory in Springfield. In the end, state militia defeated the rebels. It lasted many months because not only local town militias refused to fight the rebels and sometimes even joined them, but also because there was no national army to fund or send troops, meaning private funding was needed to get an army.

This shined a light on the weak government under the Articles because of the lack of an army to stop violence. Later on, leaders hooded the Constitutional Convention in 1787 to draft a new constitution with stronger federal government and fix other problems as well (like the Amendment process).

The articles of Confederation consists of six sheets of parchment paper stitched together. These are a brief summary of the articles' content.

  • Article I: Gave the name of the confederacy: “The United States of America”.
  • Article II: Said each state was independent and sovereign.
  • Article III: Established a “league of friendship” between states for common defense and mutual and general welfare.
  • Article IV-V: Every citizen has free ingress and regress from and to every state in the confederacy. If a citizen faced a legal matter such as a felony or misdemeanor, it will be looked into by the state which they fled from. Full faith and credit was given to the states.
  • Article VI-VIII: Restricted states from making independent foreign treaties or individual warships & standing armies without permission.
  • Article IX: Granted congress power to declare war, make peace, sign treaties, manage relations with Native Americans, coin money, and run a postal service.
  • Article X-XIII: Detailed emergency powers for committees when Congress adjourned, accepted war debt, and declared the union perpetual unless unanimously altered.

The articles established a single-branch national government. This means that there was only a one-house legislature (no House of Representatives, no senate yet), equal states votes & no separate executive or judicial branches yet (no president, no federal courts yet)

Summary & Conclusion

The Articles of Confederation were the first constitution of the U.S. after independence from Britain. They were mostly made in a way that organized the government to prevent tyranny, but this brought many problems to the well-being of the country. Violent uprisings and rebellions helped people notice these problems and later on change them. It took some time getting all states to accept the change the Articles of Confederation for the U.S. Constitution, but in the end it was ratified.
