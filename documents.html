The Declaration of Independence (1776)

Article written by A Student's Guide to Law. Last updated: June 29, 2026

The Declaration of Independence basically formally created the United States as a whole. Explore the historical context and its effects on our society

What is now the United States of America had been previously colonized by Great Britain from 1607 to 1783, beginning with the colonization of Jamestown, Virginia and concluding with the end of the American Revolutionary War and the signing of the Treaty of Paris in 1783.

British powers often took advantage of the American colonies in many ways, some examples include forcing the colonies to export raw materials from their territory to the mother country (Britain) while taxing a lot on the manufactured goods (the ones that came out of British factories) they were forced to buy back. Colonists were also prohibited from trading goods with other countries that were not Britain, therefore only enriching themselves.

A document (The Declaration of Independence) was then created to declare the 13 colonies fully independent from British powers, unify them, and secure new foreign alliances.

To do that, they needed to make the American colonies sovereign and capable of making treaties and waging war. This document was fueled by many Enlightenment principles, especially the ideas of popular sovereignty and the social contract. The document was written by Thomas Jefferson.

Now, you know why the Declaration of Independence was written. Let's look at its content and structure.

The Declaration of Independence contains 5 parts:

  • The Introduction (Preamble): This part talks about what the document is all about, declaring that they would state why they would be separating from the British Empire and also then talking about how Independence was pretty much inevitable for them.
  • The Statement of Human Rights: Here, Enlightenment principles are all over the place. The Social Contract theory by John Locke was covered in stating the right of the people to "life, liberty, and happiness" and that governments receive their power from the governed, not from monarchs or divine rights.
  • The Charges Against Human Rights: Also influenced by the Social Contract theory, this section covers the idea that it was the duty of the people to overthrow or alter a government if it was not protecting their rights, which was what Great Britain was doing to them according to the document.
  • The Statement of Separation (list of grievances against King George III): Included in detail 27 abuses by King George, such as tyranny, taxation without representation, and waging wars without consent.
  • Conclusion (The Pledge): It declared the 13 colonies independent, officialy cut ties with Britain, and "pledges the signers' lives, fortune and honor". That same sentence finalized the Declaration of Independence text itself.

Today, the Declaration of Independence still inspires many liberational movements around the world.
